Hiring Notes by Mustafa Tarabya

Written by Mustafa Tarabya, founder of CVBooster. Notes on job ads, CVs and hiring, from someone who wrote the ads and read the applications.

Read the Job Ad Like the Person Who Wrote It

The job ad you are about to answer was probably written in twenty minutes, by someone who had three other things to do that afternoon, by copying last year's ad and changing the salary. That is not a reason to despair. It is the most useful thing you will learn about it, because it tells you how to read it.

I used to write these ads myself. I ran a services company with seven people, and every time someone left I sat down, opened the old advert, fixed the dates and posted it. Then I read every application that came back. So here is how the ad looks from the other side of the desk, and what that means for the CV you send.

The ad has three layers, and only one of them matters

Every job ad mixes three kinds of text.

The furniture. "Dynamic team", "fast-paced environment", "passion for excellence". This was in the ad before the current manager was hired and it will be there after they leave. Nobody screens on it. You can ignore it completely.

The wish list. The long bullet list of nice-to-haves. Half of it was added by someone in a meeting who said "and it would be great if they also knew..." Nobody expects you to hit all of it.

The real job. Usually three or four lines, often buried in the middle. These describe what the person will actually do on a normal Tuesday. This is the part that decides who gets a call.

Your first job is to find layer three. Take a pen, or a highlighter in a PDF, and mark only the lines that describe daily work: what gets made, fixed, sold, answered or reported. Most ads have fewer of these lines than you expect.

Count the repeats

When a hiring manager cares about something, it leaks. It shows up in the title, again in the duties, and a third time in the requirements, in slightly different words. "Customer onboarding" in the summary, "onboard new clients" in the duties, "experience with onboarding" in the requirements. That is not sloppy writing. That is the manager telling you what keeps them up at night.

Write down every phrase that appears twice or more. That short list is your target. If your CV does not say those things, in close to those words, it does not matter how good the rest of it is.

Use their words, not your synonyms

You call it "client success". They call it "account management". You are describing the same work, but the screening software does not know that, and a tired recruiter on application number ninety does not have time to translate for you.

This is the single cheapest fix in job hunting. Go through your CV and, wherever you describe the same thing they describe, switch to their noun. You are not lying and you are not stuffing. You are labelling your experience in the language of the people buying it.

Put the match where it gets read

A recruiter's first look at a CV is short. What they see in that first pass is the top third of page one: your headline, your summary, and the first bullets of your most recent job. That is where your matching words need to live.

A practical order:

  1. Headline: the job title they used, if it honestly fits you. "Account Manager", not "Customer-Focused Professional".
  2. Summary: two or three lines that use two of the repeated phrases from your list.
  3. First role: the top bullet should describe the duty they care about most, with a number if you have one you can stand behind.

Everything else on the CV supports these three things. If a bullet does not help prove the real job, it is decoration, and decoration costs you attention.

What to do with the wish list

The wish list frightens people out of applying. Ten requirements, you have six, so you do not send it. Meanwhile somebody with five sends theirs and gets the interview.

Here is the rule I used when reading applications: if someone clearly had the real job covered, I did not care about the wish list. I hired people who had zero of the nice-to-haves, because the nice-to-haves were mostly nice. Cover layer three properly and treat layer two as bonus points.

Where you do have a wish-list item, mention it once, briefly, lower down. Do not build your CV around it.

Check it before you send it

Once you have tailored the CV, read the ad and your CV side by side one more time and ask three questions:

  • Does my headline match their title, or something close to it?
  • Do the phrases they repeated appear in my top third?
  • Would a stranger reading only my first five lines know I can do the Tuesday work?

If any answer is no, fix that before you fix fonts, colours or margins. Formatting has never rescued a CV that answers the wrong job.

You can do all of this with a highlighter and twenty minutes. The short version

  • Find the three or four lines that describe the real job.
  • List the phrases that repeat.
  • Use their words, and put them in the top third of page one.
  • Treat the wish list as a bonus, not a gate.

The ad was written in a hurry. Your answer to it does not have to be.
